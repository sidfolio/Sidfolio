Introduction

When we use LLM models, we assume that conversation is remembered throughout. We also think that our context and information is all within the chat window, until we realize that the long thread of conversation has too much text that you cannot comprehend anymore. As you continue texting in the chat window, we realize that it has forgotten so much information and starts hallucinating. The core problem with the LLMs is that the information we share with it and the output it generates in the first couple of turns cannot be changed. The LLMs keep going back to those interpretations and ideas. For every problem and iteration, we are expected to type or speak about what we want and cover as much detail as possible, except it becomes exhausting. In the early 20s and the web 3.0, we learnt that no one wants to read or write too much since it builds too much cognitive load, then how did we get here?

Problem Statement

How can we make context and memory in LLM tools easier to comprehend and make their user experience more efficient, so that the users can trace how their context is interpreted by AI, and make human-AI interaction more robust and intuitive.Will a non-linear approach towards interaction with LLMs make user experience efficient and intuitive?

What all the LLM tools taught us

People enjoy discussing their goals and thoughts, but only when they’re getting started. As the conversation takes turns, the LLM models start accumulating lumps of information and get overloaded with context. This is when context and memory management matters. The user should be in control of their shared context and decide how it is interpreted

REFERENCES

The advocated process of Claude is to create projects, add memory instructions and resource files. But have you observed how the memory and instructions are also long paragraphs and pure text that you have to read through? Figma weave and n8n have similar interfaces with different goals. They inspired the interface ideas for us to consider. Although these products are made for building workflows, the use of nodes do provide an insight on how contexts can be aligned better. While most LLM tools are chat-based and linear, Miro’s canvas design suggests how AI interaction does not need to be just conversational, but also mutable and interactive.

Research findings

A Northeastern University study suggests 1. 39% of average performance drops in LLM response quality when instructions arrive across multiple conversation turns instead of all at once. 2. Adding even 10% irrelevant context to the prompt reduces accuracy by 23%. 3. AI drifts away from user constraints over time. People always suggest different methods of prompt engineering such as writing a fully-fledged prompt and sharing adequate context. However, individuals tend to share context that is out of place. Conversation threads only become longer and we are told to ask LLM’s for a summary, paste it into new chats and start new conversations for better output, but it exhausts users eventually. It is important for us to understand black fog, grey fog and visible fields. Black fog is the unseen world that models don’t know exists, grey fog is stored memory that’s known and visible field is active context window where models actually reason. This insight explains that compressing old context (grey fog) to make room for new context (visible field) will push the old ones in the black fog. Which means AI either has to relearn the context or forget parts of context.

Northeastern University Research insights

A UC San Diego study highlights fixation problems in LLMs that affect creative research.
LLMs can generate hundreds of ideas, but the way we interact with them pushes us towards converging on one idea, instead of exploring the space of possibilities.
Their study proposes LLMs generating categories dimensions for structured output generation and introducing semantic zoom, where responses appear as dots, titles, keywords, summaries or full text depending on zoom level.

--------------------------------------------

While Performance drops and idea generation can still be worked around with, An MIT study highlights Sycophancy, AI’s tendency to agree with you even if you are wrong. Flatters you under the garb of feedback, and changes its response based on your input even if it was right the first time. The study suggests that sycophancy is okay until the decision is factual, political or has high stakes. The worst part of this is cleaning up context doesn’t reduce the problem, it concentrates it. The more accurately the LLM understands the user, the higher the risk of sycophancy. The design problem can be resolved by making the memory profile visible, introducing personalization controls, and self-detection within the model where the LLM models can detect whether they’re being sycophantic by comparing their contextualized response to a baseline response.

MIT RESEARCH INSIGHTS

A UC Berkley paper named, ‘Lost is the middle’, explains that performance is often highest when relevant information occurs at the beginning or end of the input context, and significantly degrades when models must access relevant information in the middle of long contexts, even for explicitly long-context models Position of the relevant information in the input context affects the performance of the LLM models, especially within long input contexts.

UC BERKLEY RESEARCH INSIGHTS

It is important to understand that since context and memory is all textual and linear, the users are not aware of how the context is moving in the chat window and how AI is interpreting the information shared by the user.

During our study, our research helped us narrow down to a design problem:

How might we make context within LLM models explicit and mutable that gives users direct control, makes users less dependent on LLMs for context interpretation and makes the LLM interaction non-linear?

A research conducted by University of Waterloo state:

Users encounter difficulties in resuming their conversations from where they left off, as the model may not consistently retain all pertinent memories
LLM models must enable users to have direct control over how they want to use memory during AI conversations

Studying the role of visualization in LLM-Assisted knowledge graph system, I learnt that we need to design for:

Active skepticism, not just passive transparency.
Mutable context with visible friction forces deliberate action.
The visualization layer brings confidence amongst users.

Identifying metaphors to represent context

In order to treat context as a first-class interaction object, we had to think of metaphors to present them. Design metaphors are conceptual bridges that connect familiar, real-world experiences to new or complex systems. Context and memory in LLM models are new and complex aspects because they vary across conversations, use cases, models and more. Currently, everything is text- conversations, context, memory, instructions, summaries and iterations. Once you have built something using Claude, Replit or Lovable, although the output can be visual, the context within the model isn't. It is hidden and the users have no control over it. Therefore, we had to come up with different metaphors to represent them. Our initial iterations were inspired by Knowledge graphs

We explored 5+ metaphors for context and memory representation

1. Knowledge graphs
2. Graphs and nodes: Nodes and connections representing relationships between context pieces. Our first iteration was inspired by this model.
3. Tiles: Context as expandable tiles. Select, drill down, expand. Inspired by Mahjong tiles. A lattice of context you can zoom into.
4. Transit Maps: Hubs are themes, stations are ideas, transfers are cross-dependencies. Spatial and navigable. You decide the route.
5. Stories: Context as narrative. Sequential with arc and progression. Makes context feel like a story being told rather than data being stored.
6. Git: Version control for context. Branching, committing, and reverting interpretations like code history.

Knowledge Graphs as a metaphor

Each piece of shared information becomes an inspectable node in the graph

Edges = Relationships

Connections between context pieces show how the AI links information together

Clusters = Themes

Maps as a metaphor

As we explored different metaphors, our goal was simple. How do you work with these models without becoming dependent on them? We took inspiration from the transit map, where hubs are the lines, ideas are the stations and cross-dependencies are transfer corridors. Even if you don't know the details, you just need to see the lines and the stops to get from point A to point B. This is a spatial, navigable view of your own project so you stay oriented. You're the one who decides
